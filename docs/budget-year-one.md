# Budgeting for the First Year of Repairs

The most expensive surprise in homeownership is the one you didn't budget for. Here's a realistic, calm framework for your first 12 months: what a house actually costs per month beyond the mortgage, and a repair fund you can actually sustain.

*Education only — not financial advice. Your numbers will vary by house, region, and luck; this chapter gives you the structure so your numbers have somewhere to live.*

## The real monthly payment (it's four numbers, not one)

| Number | What it is | How to estimate it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Principal & interest | Your actual loan payment | On your Loan Estimate / Closing Disclosure |
| Taxes | Property taxes, often escrowed monthly | County records for the property (find recent assessment × rate) |
| Insurance | Homeowner's policy | Get real quotes pre-offer, not post-closing guesses |
| Maintenance + repairs | The fund you set aside yourself | Often quoted at 1–2% of home value annually |

A common rookie mistake: budgeting "principal + interest" and treating everything else as "probably fine." Taxes and insurance alone regularly add 25–45% onto the loan payment, and maintenance is the line item people skip entirely.

## The 1% rule (and when to use 2%)

The classic guideline: save **1% of the home's value per year** for maintenance and repairs. A $300,000 house = $250/month in your repair fund.

Use 2% instead when any of these apply:

- The house is over ~40 years old (older roof, plumbing, electrical, furnace all closer to their replacement windows).
- The inspection flagged "end of service life" for any major system.
- The roof is at its last few years, or the furnace/water heater has no service history.

Use less only if you have a genuine, documented safety net — and even then, keep at least $2,000 liquid from day one, because plumbing does not respect payday cycles.

## Your Year One punch list

The first year is about **finding and fixing**, not renovating. Sequence priorities like this:

| Priority | What | Typical first-year cost range |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Safety first | Smoke/CO detectors, GFCI outlets in wet rooms, loose handrails, gas-water-heater strapping | $50–300 (diy-most) |
| Water second | Downspout extensions, gutter cleanout, small roof fixes, grading/soil correction, sump-pump check | $100–800 |
| Critical-systems third | Furnace/AC **tune-ups first** ($100–200 each — inspections before repairs), water heater flush, chimney sweep if used | $200–500 |
| Everything else | Caulking, weatherstripping, paint touch-ups, filter changes, door/hardware fixes as items appear | $200–500 |

## A simple monthly plan that actually works

Set up three accounts (or three labeled sub-accounts at your bank):

1. **Bills account:** the full mortgage payment (P&I + escrowed taxes/insurance) + insurance non-escrowed, if applicable.
2. **Repair fund:** transfer **$X/month** (your 1–2% rule number) automatically on payday, not at month-end.
3. **Buffer:** one month of *all* house costs (mortgage + utilities), built up over your first year if you don't already have it.

The magic is automation, not discipline. What's automatic gets funded; what needs monthly willpower gets skipped during a busy month, and busy months **always** come in year one — new job stresses, new commute, new everything.

## A realistic sample budget

For a $300,000 house, 20% down, in a mid-cost region — illustrative only:

- Mortgage P&I (240k, 30yr @ ~6.5%): **~$1,517**
- Property taxes (escrowed): **~$250** *(your county's rate decides; can be 2–3× this elsewhere)*
- Insurance: **~$150** *(varies widely by region/risk)*
- Repair fund (1%): **$250**
- **Total house cost: ~$2,167/month** — which is why "what's my payment?" only ever gets you part of the way.

Then add utilities (heat/water/electric/trash — often $150–400/month, more in extreme climates and older homes), internet, and any HOA dues.

## The Year One repair ladder (rank fixes by consequence, not by noise)

1. **Anything that lets water in or out wrongly** — roof leaks, failed sump pumps, broken pipes, water heater drips, sewer backup.
2. **Anything where failure hurts a person** — gas leaks, smoke detectors, loose stair railings, gas range not vented.
3. **Anything that gets worse fast if ignored** — a $15 gutter fix beating a $4,000 fascia/soffit rot repair, that kind of math.
4. **Cosmetic items** — in order of the joy-to-cost ratio. Paint is high on that list; if it also makes you happy every morning, it earns its keep.

## When the inspection “future risks” list comes due

Your inspection report likely mentioned some "in coming years" items. Roughly plan (varies widely by region and home size):

- Roof replacement: **$8,000–20,000+** (large homes, metal, or steep pitches cost more)
- Furnace/AC replacement: **$5,000–12,000** each or paired
- Water heater: **$1,200–2,500**, tankless often more
- Repipe (aged galvanized/polybutylene): **$5,000–15,000+**
- Electrical panel upgrade: **$1,500–4,000**
- Exterior paint / siding repair: **$2,000–10,000**

If any of these are 2–3 years out rather than 10, budget toward them separately rather than letting them land on a credit card "some day." Reasonable rule of thumb: divide the estimated cost by the months of life remaining, and that's the amount that goes into the repair fund alongside your base 1%.
