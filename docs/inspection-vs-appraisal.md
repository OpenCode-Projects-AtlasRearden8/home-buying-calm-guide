# Inspection vs. Appraisal, Decoded

If you ask a room of first-time buyers to define these two words, you'll get answers that cross-contaminate. Here's the clean separation, once and for all.

*Education only. Your agent and lender will make recommendations specific to your deal; this guide just draws the map.*

## The one-line difference

- **Inspection:** done **for you**, to find out what's physically wrong with the house.
- **Appraisal:** done **for the lender**, to find out what the house is financially worth.

Two people, two different jobs, two different reports, both mandatory in practice (the inspection is technically optional, but skipping it is a gamble beyond the scope of this manual).

## Side by side

| Question | Home inspection | Appraisal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who orders it | You (and pays for it, typically $300–500) | Your lender (you pay via the loan fee sheet) |
| Who it protects | **You** | **The lender** |
| What it answers | "What's wrong with this house?" | "Is this house worth the price we're lending against?" |
| What it looks at | Roof, systems, structure's visible parts, moisture, safety hazards, every room | Overall condition, features, size, recent nearby sales (comps) |
| What you receive | A report — often dozens of pages with photos | A valuation — a number, with comps and adjustments |
| Result options | Negotiate repairs/credit, walk away under contingency, or accept | Value ≥ price → proceed; value < price → renegotiate, pay difference, or exercise appraisal contingency |
| Typical timing | Right after your offer is accepted (first 7–10 days) | 1–3 weeks after you go under contract |
| What it does NOT do | Values the house; instead, inspects it in detail | Finds all the defects of the house; appraisers walk through, not through subfloors |
| When it happens | Once, before you commit | Once, before the lender commits |

## What the inspection experience is actually like

1. **You pick and schedule the inspector** — usually right after your offer is accepted, and yes, you should attend the inspection in person (allow 2–3 hours).
2. The inspector works through a system checklist: exterior, roof, plumbing, electrical, HVAC, interior, attic, crawlspace.
3. **Ask them to show you anything they flag.** Most inspectors love explaining; you'll learn your future house's vocabulary.
4. You get a report within about 24 hours, with severity levels — not everything found is actually a problem.
5. Now you have **a document that generates negotiating leverage**, inside the deadline your purchase agreement set.

A common first-time mistake: reading the report like homework with a grade. It's more like a dot-by-dot map of your next ten years of house work. The point isn't to find a "flawless house," it's to know what you're signing up for.

## What the appraisal experience is actually like

1. The lender orders it; you mostly wait.
2. An appraiser visits, measures, photographs, and compares your target to recent nearby sales — "comps."
3. You get a number. It's (usually) the price you agreed to, or close to it.
4. If the number is lower, you have decisions — see below.

The appraiser is not looking for "cracked tiles" (that's your inspector's job) so much as "is a 3-bed/2-bath/1,600 sq ft house here worth $340,000, given that three similar houses nearby sold for $335,000-$345,000 last quarter?"

## When the appraisal comes in low

Three normal paths, in order of commonness:

1. **Renegotiate to the appraised value.** Sellers sometimes meet you there, because if the deal breaks, the next offer will hit the same wall.
2. **Split the difference.** Some middle number between price and appraisal.
3. **Pay the difference in cash**, or walk away under the appraisal contingency. Paying the gap is a real option — but it needs to fit the budget, and lenders basing the loan on the appraised number may ask for the difference in cash.

*What's wise in your specific situation is a live question for your agent; knowing that all three paths exist is the whole point of this chapter.*

## Frequently confused, quickly settled

- **"The inspector's estimate of repairs matched the appraisal."** Coincidence of vocabulary, different documents.
- **"The appraiser found Mold."** Appraisers note obvious condition issues; they don't crawl under your house.
- **"I can pick my own appraiser."** Generally no — the lender selects the appraiser to keep the process independent. You pick the inspector; you pick the closing agent.
- **"Do I need an inspection if the house is new construction?"** Builders make mistakes, and a new-home inspection exists for this reason. Many buyers in new builds still inspect, just as they would with a 1988 ranch.
- **"Does the appraisal tell me what I should offer?"** No — it's a lag value of the market. Your offer price is set by you, your agent, and the market, not by an appraiser who arrives after the fact.

## Your inspection contingency in four deadlines

Check the purchase agreement, but roughly:

1. **Offer accepted** → schedule the inspection within 3–5 days.
2. **Inspection happens** → you receive the report in 1–2 days.
3. **Deadlines in the contract** → submit either a request for repairs/credit, or a walk-away, *in writing and before the date on your contract*.
4. **Deadline passes with no action** → you generally lose the protection (some agreements auto-extend; don't rely on memory for this).

Write these dates into a shared calendar the moment your offer is signed — with a reminder two days before each deadline, because that's how people avoid missing them by accident.
