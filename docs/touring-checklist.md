# Touring With a Checklist (Not Vibes)

Falling in love with a house takes about ninety seconds. Spotting what's wrong with it takes a plan. This chapter gives you the plan: a systematic tour checklist so you come home with real observations instead of feelings and two identical kitchens you can't tell apart.

*Education only — this checklist helps you take good notes; it doesn't replace a professional inspection.*

## Before you tour anything

- [ ] Decide your **musts, wants, and dealbreakers** in writing. If it's not written down, it doesn't exist by day four of touring.
- [ ] Set your **top monthly payment** (with taxes + insurance) and your **top purchase price** carrying it. Write both numbers in your phone notes.
- [ ] Charge your phone fully; you'll photograph a lot.
- [ ] Buy an outlet tester (~$10 at any hardware store, no electrical knowledge needed). It is the single best $10 in house hunting.

Tour rules that keep you sane:

1. **Max 4 houses per day.** After that, they blur together. Take ten minutes between houses and type your notes in the car before driving off.
2. **Same tour route in every house:** outside → kitchen → bathrooms → bedrooms → basement/attic → yard. Consistency makes comparisons honest.
3. **Never say "I love it" out loud.** Feel free to feel it; save the words for the car.

## The Tour Checklist

### 1. Street and neighborhood (do this first, in the car)

- [ ] Walk/drive three blocks in each direction. What's the general upkeep of the block?
- [ ] Commute test at your real commute hour, if at all possible.
- [ ] Noise check: highway? Flight path? Train tracks? Sunday-morning church bells count too.
- [ ] Neighbors' yards: standing water, junk cars, or signs of local issues (flood/storm damage)?
- [ ] Cell signal in the driveway — you actually use it every day.
- [ ] Street parking: is there any, and where would guests put a car?

### 2. Outside

- [ ] **Grading:** does the ground slope *away* from the house? Flat-toward or toward-the-house = water questions.
- [ ] Gutters and downspouts: present, attached, and ending somewhere sensible?
- [ ] Roof from ground: are shingles curling, missing, or patchy? How old does it look? (Ask for the roof age in writing.)
- [ ] Chimney: leaning? Cracked mortar?
- [ ] Siding: cracked stucco, warped boards, peeling paint?
- [ ] Foundation: stair-step cracks in brick, horizontal cracks in poured walls, or a visible lean.
- [ ] Trees: big branches overhanging the roof, or trees within throwing distance of it.
- [ ] Fence lines vs. property lines — check against the survey if one exists.
- [ ] Driveway: big cracks, settling, or pooling water.

### 3. Entry, layout, and general

- [ ] Door frames: are any doors sticking or not closing (settling clue)?
- [ ] Look up from every room. Stains on ceilings matter more than paint colors.
- [ ] Look down. Squeaky-floored, sprung tiles, or spongy spots near tubs and sinks?
- [ ] Windows: condensation *between* panes (failed seal), painted-shut, or cracked.
- [ ] Smell: fresh paint covering a musty basement is the classic tell. If a room smells aggressively of air freshener, quietly ask why.
- [ ] Age of big systems (ask the agent, and ask for it in writing): furnace, A/C, water heater, roof.

### 4. Kitchen

- [ ] Run the water, run the faucet, drain both. Slow-draining kitchen sinks are a common, telling defect.
- [ ] Under the sink: any warped wood, stains, or a smell of previous leaks?
- [ ] Counters level? Cabinets open without a fight? Drawer runners intact?
- [ ] Outlets: GFCI near water? (Use the outlet tester here.)
- [ ] Appliance ages and brands (sticker on the door frame or inside the oven door often shows the year).
- [ ] Fridge space: does a standard fridge actually fit in the cutout?

### 5. Bathrooms

- [ ] **Threat level test:** run hot water and cold at the same time and flush the toilet. Does the shower turn into lava?
- [ ] **Grout and caulk:** black spots = sophomore-level mold. Regrouting is cheap; a rotted subfloor under a tub isn't.
- [ ] Toilet: does it wobble? Push gently on each corner.
- [ ] Under every sink, cabinet open, look for the same stains you looked for in the kitchen.
- [ ] Bathroom exhaust fans: do they vent *somewhere sensible* (outside), or just into the attic?

### 6. Bedrooms and living areas

- [ ] Outlets in each room (old homes sometimes have a single outlet per wall).
- [ ] Squeaks when walking — fun to note, not structural, but employees of your future sleep will care.
- [ ] Closet and storage: is there enough, does anyone plan to park a car in the garage it used to hold?
- [ ] Where is the HVAC register air actually flowing? Rooms far from the furnace often run cold.

### 7. Basement / crawlspace / attic

- [ ] Basement: look at the *edges* of the floor for water lines, white mineral crust (efflorescence), and standing dust in the corners.
- [ ] Sniff test: musty = "moisture event" at some point. Imagination runs wild; moisture never leaves.
- [ ] Water heater: age on the sticker, any rust near the bottom, any leak onto the drain pan?
- [ ] Furnace: age, and any service stickers on it? A maintained unit has a decade of little service dates on it.
- [ ] Crawlspace: can you see standing water or mud? (Use your phone's light and take photos from the door rather than crawling.)
- [ ] Attic (if accessed): insulation level, daylight where there shouldn't be any, and rhodamine-sized stains on the underside of the roof deck.

### 8. Final sweep (60 seconds, in the hall before you leave)

- [ ] Outlet-test 3 random outlets.
- [ ] Take a photo of every wall where you noticed anything.
- [ ] Photograph the basement corners, the roof from the curb, and the furnace/water heater stickers.
- [ ] Type three sentences in your phone: **top 3 worries**, **top 1 joy**, **price I'd offer** — while it's all fresh.

## The "I noticed this, what do I do with it" triage

| What you noticed | What it usually means | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hairline cracks above door corners | Normal settling | Note it, mention to inspector |
| Wide, horizontal, or stair-step cracks | Possible structural movement | Hire an inspection; get photos |
| Musty basement smell or water lines | Past moisture event | Ask when, change extension of downspouts plan, expect inspection to dig |
| Water stains on ceilings | Active or old roof/plumbing leak | Ask when and what was repaired; verify via inspection |
| One wonky door or window | Settling or bad hardware | Low priority |
| Doors and windows misaligned across several rooms | Possible foundation movement | Mid-priority; photograph |
| Power to an outlet tester: "open ground" | Old wiring, easy fix per outlet | Note number of affected outlets |
| Breaker panel is a Federal Pacific or Zinsco brand | Older panel with known safety history | Inspector should flag; factor a panel replacement |
| Bathroom sink drains slow | Hair. Usually just hair | Relax |
| Sloped floors | Old house character \or\ framing issue | Height of deflection matters; ask inspector |

## Comparing houses like a pro

After two or three tours, homes dissolve into each other unless you score them. Do it simply:

- **Dealbreakers met?** (Y/N first — nothing else matters until this is settled.)
- **Top worries:** list 0–3 items from your triage above.
- **Real monthly cost guess** = ask + taxes + insurance + utilities (old houses cost more to heat; ask to see average bills).
- **Gut score 1–10.** Then ignore the gut score until after the other three, so it's a tie-breaker, not the whole decision.

The winners will emerge on their own; the checklist just gives you the data to trust.
